# Resources

## Research Tools

These are tools researchers use to find, collect, or explore social media data. Many of them come from the Social Media Lab's [Social Media Toolkit](https://socialmedialab.ca/apps/social-media-toolkit/), an older, non-exhaustive list of open access tools that is worth browsing for more options. All of them are free to use and none requires coding. Because they are free, they are limited in what they can collect, as we talked about in class, so read each tool's own documentation before you rely on it.

* **Zeeschuimer**
    * [Link](https://github.com/digitalmethodsinitiative/zeeschuimer)
    * A Firefox browser extension from the Digital Methods Initiative that saves the data a site sends to your browser while you scroll, so you can collect posts from platforms like TikTok, Instagram, and X without an API. You can export what it captures as a spreadsheet-friendly file or send it to the analysis tool 4CAT. It only collects what you load, and it can't capture some views, like Instagram Stories.
* **YouTube Data Tools: Video Comments**
    * [Link](https://ytdt.digitalmethods.net/video-comments)
    * Enter a YouTube video ID and this tool downloads the video's comments along with basic information about the video. It is one of several modules in YouTube Data Tools, built by Bernhard Rieder at the Digital Methods Initiative. The tool's own documentation warns that its data handling may not comply with GDPR, so stick to public videos and think about what you keep.
* **OSoMeNet**
    * [Link](https://osome.iu.edu/tools/osomenet/)
    * A tool from Indiana University's Observatory on Social Media that draws diffusion graphs showing who amplified what, and how often, through replies, reposts, mentions, or hashtags. It works with data from Bluesky, Mastodon, TikTok, and the Observatory's own archive. Useful if you're interested in how information spreads and which communities form around it.
* **Meta Ad Library (political and issue ads)**
    * [Link](https://business.facebook.com/ads/library/?active_status=all&ad_type=all&country=ALL&is_targeted_country=false&media_type=all&sort_data[mode]=total_impressions&sort_data[direction]=desc)
    * Meta's public archive of ads that ran on Facebook and Instagram. This link searches active political and social issue ads in the US, sorted by total impressions, and the library shows who paid for each ad. Useful if you're interested in political advertising or how campaigns try to reach people.
* **SteamID.uk**
    * [Link](https://steamid.uk/)
    * Enter a Steam ID or profile URL to see an account's past avatars and names, ban records, and a reputation score. Useful if you're interested in gaming communities, but it looks up individual people's accounts, so be careful with identifiable information.
* **SOMAR: Social Media Archive at ICPSR**
    * [Link](https://www.icpsr.umich.edu/sites/somar/search/studies?q=)
    * A collection of public and restricted social media datasets, held at the University of Michigan's ICPSR data archive, that you can search by topic. Restricted data requires an application and is accessed through a secure virtual environment. Useful if you'd rather start from data that other researchers already collected and documented than collect your own.
* **Communalytic**
    * [Link](https://communalytic.org/)
    * A platform for studying online communities that can collect from Bluesky and Reddit for free and others for a fee. It can also use data from a spreadsheet you upload. It includes built-in analysis of networks, sentiment, topics, and toxic or uncivil comments. 
* **Harvard Dataverse**
    * [Link](https://dataverse.harvard.edu/dataverse/harvard?q=social+media&types=dataverses%3Adatasets%3Afiles&sort=score&order=desc)
    * A free, open repository where researchers from any field share their datasets, and this link searches it for "social media." Useful if you'd rather start from data that other researchers already collected than collect your own. Check each dataset's description and any conditions on its use before relying on it.
* **Applied Archival Downloading with Wget**
    * [Link](https://programminghistorian.org/en/lessons/applied-archival-downloading-with-wget)
    * A tutorial from Programming Historian on using Wget to download whole or parts of websites. 
* **Automated Downloading with Wget**
    * [Link](https://programminghistorian.org/en/lessons/automated-downloading-with-wget)
    * A tutorial from Programming Historian on using Wget to download whole or parts of websites. 

## Examples of Research on Internet and Social Media Culture
 
These are real studies of the internet and how it shapes behavior. You are not expected to produce anything like their scale. Read them to see how researchers pick an object of study, decide what counts as data, explain their method, and admit their limits. Those are the same moves your prospectus asks for.
 
What to pay attention to: Start with the abstract, skim the introduction, then find the data and methods section and ask:
 
* What did they actually collect? (How many comments, posts, videos, or people? From where, and when?)
* How did they decide what counts? (Categories, codes, or interview questions)
* What did they find and what couldn't they find?

**A note before you start:** Many of these papers are complex. They were written by professional researchers for other researchers, often with training in statistics, programming, or theory that you don't have yet, and that's fine. You are not expected to understand every paper, and you don't need to follow the methods or the math. Use them for ideas and framing: what questions people ask about the internet, what counts as data, how they explain what they did, etc.

* **"TikTok, Do Your Thing": User Reactions to Social Surveillance in the Public Sphere**

    * Authors: Meira Gilbert, Miranda Wei, Lindah Kotut - [Link](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.20884)
    * Hand-coding comments and videos about a TikTok trend where users film strangers in public and crowdsource identifying them
    * Collected 60 videos and 1,901 comments
    * You did this on a smaller scale in the week 6 lab

* **"This is so Vine coded": Genre, nostalgia, and strategies of multimodal intertextuality on TikTok**

    * Author: Kendra Calhoun - [Link](https://www.jbe-platform.com/content/journals/10.1075/ip.00143.cal)
    * Hand-coding TikToks and their comments to see how users refer back to the defunct app Vine and describe videos as having "Vine energy"
    * Collected 201 TikToks and their comments
    * A good example of a small sample and a playful topic that still makes an argument

* **Recommending Toxicity: The role of algorithmic recommender functions on YouTube Shorts and TikTok in promoting male supremacist influencers**

    * Authors: Catherine Baker, Debbie Ging, Maja Brandt Andreasen - [Link](https://www.dcu.ie/antibullyingcentre/recommending-toxicity-summary-report)
    * An audit of whether TikTok and YouTube Shorts steer young men toward misogynistic "manosphere" content, using 10 test accounts on new phones
    * Watched and hand-coded over 29 hours of recommended videos
    * Shows how researchers study a personalized feed and it is upfront about its small sample, similar to your week 6 homework

* **Algorithmic Folk Theories and Identity: How TikTok Users Co-Produce Knowledge of Identity and Engage in Algorithmic Resistance**

    * Authors: Nadia Karizat, Dan Delmonaco, Motahhare Eslami, Nazanin Andalibi - [Link](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3476046)
    * Interviews about how TikTok users think the For You Page works and how those beliefs change what they post
    * Interviewed 15 US-based TikTok users
    * Example of how to collect data about the internet and social media through interviews rather than comments and the like

* **My Favorite Streamer is an LLM: Discovering, Bonding, and Co-Creating in AI VTuber Fandom**

    * Authors: Jiayi Ye, Chaoran Chen, Yue Huang, Yanfang Ye, Toby Jia-Jun Li, Xiangliang Zhang - [Link](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.10427)
    * A mixed study of fans of Neuro-sama, an AI streamer, and how they bond with it
    * Surveyed 334 fans, interviewed 12, and analyzed more than 550,000 chat messages from livestreams
    * An example of studying the same question from several angles at once

* **To Use or Not to Use: Investigating Student Perceptions of Faculty Generative AI Usage in Higher Education**

    * Authors: Jie Gao, Jiayi Zhang, Dan Chen - [Link](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25932)
    * A survey asking students whether students and professors should use generative AI
    * Surveyed 156 undergraduate and graduate students, with an open-ended question about concerns
    * Relevant to you as students at the beginning of the AI age

* **A Content Analysis of Gambling Operators' Twitter Accounts at the Start of the English Premier League Football Season**

    * Authors: Elizabeth A. Killick, Mark D. Griffiths - [Link](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7026221)
    * Content analysis of how ten UK betting companies used Twitter on the opening weekend of a football season
    * Collected 3,375 tweets
    * Useful if you're interested in online marketing, gambling, or sports

* **An Empirical Content Analysis of Posts Made to the r/depression Subreddit**

    * Author: Kurt Marais - [Link](https://upjournals.up.ac.za/index.php/dhasa/article/view/5419)
    * A content analysis of posts to a large mental health community on Reddit, looking at engagement, posting times, common words, and the ages and locations of users
    * Collected 11,975 posts by 9,593 accounts over 56 days
    * Useful if you're interested in mental health or online communities, and it raises ethics questions about studying vulnerable groups

* **Social Interaction, Support Preferences, and the Use of Wearable Health Trackers**

    * Authors: Natalie Pennington, Linda Dam - [Link](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/communication/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2023.1256452/full)
    * Two online surveys on how social factors, like comparing yourself to others and sharing data, relate to how often people use fitness trackers and smartwatches
    * Surveyed 333 university students and 319 adults
    * Useful if you're interested in wearables, fitness, or social comparison
